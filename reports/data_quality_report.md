## 3. Method

**Tools:** Python 3.13.17, pandas 3.1.0.

**Checks performed**

1. Shape, column names and inferred data types.
2. Missing values (count and percentage).
3. Exact duplicate rows and duplicate `booking_id` values.
4. Date parsing, date range and time-slot validity.
5. Text consistency: city spellings, case and whitespace.
6. Numeric ranges against business rules (not statistical outliers).
7. Revenue-formula consistency.
8. Show-level consistency: one movie, theatre, screen, capacity, date and time
   per `show_id`.
9. Occupancy bounds, calculated correctly at show level.
10. Customer-level consistency: one name, gender and city per `customer_id`.
11. A first look at New / Returning / Loyal segments.

**Design principle:** a value is only "invalid" if it breaks a stated business
rule. A high price or a low-occupancy show is investigated, not automatically
deleted.

## 5. Detailed findings

### 5.1 Duplicates

**What we found**

- 10,300 rows, 10,000 distinct `booking_id` values.
- All 300 extra rows are **exact duplicates** (every field identical).
- Example: `BK0000095` appears twice with the same customer, show, seats and
  total.

**Why it matters**

Duplicates do not just inflate the booking count. They inflate:

- confirmed tickets: 23,603 vs 22,920 (+2.98%);
- confirmed revenue: ₹11,161,531 vs ₹10,823,192 (+3.12%);
- mean occupancy: 42.4% vs 41.2%.

**Treatment**

Drop rows with a repeated `booking_id`, keeping the first occurrence. Because
the copies are identical, keeping first or last does not change any value.

**Validation**

`COUNT(*) = 10,000` and `COUNT(DISTINCT booking_id) = 10,000`.

---

### 5.2 Missing values

| Column | Missing | % of rows | Recoverable? | How |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| `customer_email` | 62 | 0.60% | Yes | Same customer's other booking |
| `phone` | 60 | 0.58% | Yes | Same customer's other booking |
| `age_group` | 50 | 0.49% | Yes | Derived from `age` |
| `promo_code` | 51 | 0.50% | Mostly | Infer from `discount_amount`; else `NONE` |

223 rows have at least one blank (some rows have more than one).

**Why not fill everything with zero or "Unknown"?**

- A blank email is not the string "Unknown"; copying the same customer's email
  preserves the join to that customer.
- A blank `age_group` is fully determined by `age`, so filling it with a
  default would create a contradiction.
- A blank `promo_code` with a non-zero discount is evidence that a promo was
  used. The discount rate identifies which one, because the four promo rates
  (5%, 10%, 15%, 20%) are unique.

**Treatment**

- Email/phone: copy from another booking of the same `customer_id`. If a
  customer had only one booking, use a deterministic placeholder
  (`<customer_id>@example.com` / `0000000000`) and log it. The injected blanks
  were placed only on repeat customers, so the placeholder should not be
  needed.
- `age_group`: re-derive from the repaired `age`.
- `promo_code`: if `discount_amount > 0`, calculate
  `discount ÷ (ticket_price × seats)` and map 5/10/15/20% to
  `FIRSTBUY5`/`WEEKEND10`/`STUDENT15`/`FAMILY20`. Otherwise set `NONE`.

**Validation**

Zero blanks in all four columns; every `age_group` matches its `age`; no
discount without a promo code.

### 5.3 Prices and revenue

**What we found**

- 52 rows have `ticket_price` of 0, −50 or −100.
- 52 rows fail `total_amount = ticket_price × seats + convenience_fee − discount_amount`.
- **All 52 formula failures are the same 52 bad-price rows.** No other row
  fails the formula.

**Why it matters**

A negative price produces negative revenue and can flip a city or movie from
profitable to loss-making. It also breaks any average ticket price.

**Treatment**

Because the other terms are intact, the original price is recoverable exactly:

```
ticket_price = (total_amount − convenience_fee + discount_amount) ÷ seats
```

Then recompute `total_amount` from the recovered price and verify the formula.

**Validation**

- 0 rows with `ticket_price <= 0`.
- 0 rows where the recomputed total differs from the stored total.
- Confirmed revenue returns to **₹10,823,192**, matching the generator's clean
  base.

---

### 5.4 Dates and times

**What we found**

- 21 rows have `show_date = 2027-12-31`, outside the 2025-10-01 → 2026-09-30
  window.
- 31 rows have an invalid `show_time` (values such as `25:30`, `abc`, `99:00`).
- 0 bookings with a **valid** time were made after their show started.
- 4,056 bookings were made on the same calendar day as the show, which is
  normal for cinema.

**Why it matters**

`show_date` drives every monthly trend and `show_time` drives peak/off-peak
analysis. An impossible value silently drops a row from a month or a slot.

**Treatment**

Both fields are **show-level** attributes. For each bad row, use the modal
value for the same `show_id` from the other bookings of that show. Every
affected show has at least three bookings, so the mode is unambiguous.

**Validation**

- 0 invalid `show_time` values.
- 0 `show_date` values outside the period.
- 0 bookings made after their show starts.

---

### 5.5 Ratings and ages

**What we found**

- 10 rows have a `rating` of −1.0 or 12.5.
- 10 rows have an `age` of −5 or 150.
- In both cases, the correct value exists elsewhere in the file: the movie's
  other bookings and the customer's other bookings.

**Treatment**

- Rating: modal rating for the same `movie_title`.
- Age: the customer's age from another booking. If a customer had only one
  booking, use the median age and log it. The injected defects were placed only
  on repeat customers.

**Validation**

- 0 ratings outside 0–10.
- 0 ages outside 18–68.
- `age_group` re-derived from the repaired age.

### 5.6 City labels

**What we found**

- 34 distinct raw labels for 8 real cities.
- The Bengaluru variants alone: `Bengaluru`, `Bangalore`, `Banglore`,
  `bengaluru`, `BENGALURU`, ` Bengaluru `.
- 49 rows have leading/trailing whitespace; 114 are not in title case.
- 242 rows (2.35%) change after normalization.

**Why it matters**

`groupby("city")` treats each spelling as a separate city. In v1 of this
dataset, `Bengaluru`, `Bangalore` and `Banglore` were generated as three
**equally weighted** cities, which made Bengaluru look three times larger than
it was. v2 fixes the root cause: Bengaluru has one weight and the variants are
only labels.

**Treatment**

`str.strip()` → `str.title()` → map `Bangalore`/`Banglore` to `Bengaluru`.

**Validation**

Exactly 8 canonical city labels; every customer has one city across all of
their bookings.

### 5.7 Show-level consistency

**What we found**

| Check | Result |
|---|---|
| Shows | 290 |
| Shows with >1 movie | 0 |
| Shows with >1 theatre | 0 |
| Shows with >1 screen | 0 |
| Shows with >1 capacity | 0 |
| Shows with >1 `show_date` | 20 |
| Shows with >1 `show_time` | 30 |

The 20 date conflicts and 30 time conflicts are the same corrupted rows from
section 5.4. No show has conflicting movie, theatre, screen or capacity.

**Why it matters**

Occupancy needs one unambiguous capacity and one date/time per show. If a show
had two capacities, the denominator would be undefined.

**Treatment**

Repair the date/time using the modal value for the `show_id`. No show loses all
its bookings, so no show disappears.

**Validation**

0 shows with more than one date, time, movie, theatre, screen or capacity.

### 5.8 Occupancy

**Calculation**

```
occupancy(show) = SUM(seats of Confirmed bookings for the show) ÷ seats_capacity
```

Cancelled bookings are excluded because the seats were released. The metric is
calculated **per show**, not per booking.

**Results**

| Dataset | Min | Mean | Max | Shows > 100% |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Deduplicated | 10.3% | **41.2%** | 90.7% | 0 |
| Raw (with duplicates) | 10.3% | 42.4% | 100.0% | 0 |

No show crosses 100% in this sample because the fullest show has 9.3%
headroom. The duplicate rows still overstate the mean and the confirmed-seat
total. In a busier sample, the same bug would produce impossible values above
100%, which is why the check matters.

**Treatment**

Deduplicate first; exclude `Cancelled`; store the result as
`show_occupancy_rate` on the cleaned row (identical for every booking of the
same show).

**Validation**

0 occupancy values outside 0–1.

### 5.9 Customer consistency and segments

**What we found**

- 3,499 distinct customers.
- 0 customers with more than one name.
- 0 customers with more than one gender.
- 0 customers with more than one city **after normalization**.
- Bookings per customer (deduplicated): mean 2.87, median 2, maximum 17.

Segments are measured **after deduplication**, because a duplicated row is not
an extra visit.

| Segment | Rule | Customers | % of customers | Bookings | % of bookings |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| New | 1 booking | 1,090 | 31.3% | 1,090 | 10.9% |
| Returning | 2–3 bookings | 1,507 | 43.3% | 3,581 | 35.8% |
| Loyal | 4+ bookings | 883 | 25.4% | 5,329 | 53.3% |

**Why this had to be deduplicated:** on the raw file the same table reads
Loyal = 932 customers (26.8%) and 5,684 bookings (55.2%). Duplicates promote
49 customers from New/Returning into a higher segment and add 355 bookings to
the Loyal row. The error is small, but it is the same class of mistake as the
occupancy overstatement in section 5.8.

**Interpretation**

Loyal customers are about a quarter of customers but generate more than half
of bookings. This is the kind of concentration a retention campaign would
target. It is a **snapshot over the 12-month window**, not a lifetime-value
label: a customer with one booking in the window may have been active before
it.

**Validation**

0 customer-level name/gender/city conflicts; segment counts sum to exactly
10,000 deduplicated bookings.

## 8. Limitations

1. **Synthetic by design.** No result can be compared with a real cinema chain,
   a real film's box office, or a real customer's behaviour. Four recent release
   names are used as labels only.
2. **Sample, not census.** 10,000 bookings across 290 shows. Only shows with at
   least one sampled booking appear; occupancy is the occupancy of those shows.
   The generator simulated complete shows and kept 10,000 bookings, so measured
   occupancy (41.2%) is marginally below the aggregate base (42.1%).
3. **City mix is illustrative.** City volumes come from a prior that roughly
   reflects multiplex market size, then are sampled. They are not a market
   estimate.
4. **Ratings are static.** Each movie has one rating for the whole period, so
   the data cannot show how ratings changed over time.
5. **No cost data.** Revenue is ticket revenue; the dataset cannot calculate
   profit, margin or marketing ROI.
6. **Cancellations are simplified.** A cancellation is a status, not a refund
   with a timestamp or a fee.
7. **One booking channel per booking.** The data cannot show a customer
   starting on one channel and finishing on another.
